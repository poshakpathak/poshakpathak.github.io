Bat speed ages well. Contact quality doesn't.

Three seasons of Statcast bat tracking show which swings hold up as hitters age, and which hitters' 2026 results ran ahead of or behind the quality of their swings.

League context

The average hitter swung about half a mph faster in 2026 than in 2024, while swing length stayed flat at 7.3 feet. Hover over a bar for the exact average and the number of hitters.

How swings age

Swings change very little from year to year. Bat speed and swing length repeat almost perfectly from one season to the next, while outcomes like wOBA bounce around much more.

Hitters 24 and under add about 0.6 mph of bat speed a year. The gains shrink to 0.1 to 0.2 mph from 25 to 30, and the first average loss shows up at 31 to 33. Contact quality falls off sooner and faster. xwOBA is already slipping by 3 to 6 points a year from 25 to 30, and by 10 to 15 points after 31. Switch the chart to xwOBA to compare. The early decline comes mostly from swing decisions and contact, not from losing raw speed.

Which swing profiles hold up

Bat speed is the trait that protects a hitter. Each dot below is one hitter going from one season to the next, placed by bat speed and by how their next-season xwOBA compared with what their age and prior xwOBA predicted. Faster bats sit higher. On its own, each extra mph is worth about — points. Holding swing length, squared-up rate and whiff rate fixed as well, it is about 2.5 points, while those three traits add nothing once bat speed is known.

Splitting hitters at the median bat speed and swing length shows the same pattern. Fast swingers beat expectations in both age groups and slow swingers fell short. Long versus compact shows no consistent direction, and among hitters 30 and older the fast, long swings (mostly big power bats) held up best.

The fastest bats do lose a little speed the next year and the slowest gain a little, but that is ordinary regression to the mean, not a sign that fast swings fade faster. When projecting a hitter in their 30s, weight bat speed heavily, ignore swing length, and expect contact quality to fall faster than bat speed.

Lucky and unlucky hitters

Each hitter gets two expectations: Statcast's xwOBA, and a model that predicts wOBA from the swing itself (bat speed, swing length, squared-up and blast rates, whiffs) plus contact and plate discipline (exit velocity, launch angle, barrels, hard-hit rate, pull rate, K% and BB%). The luck score is actual wOBA minus the average of the two. Both expectations predict next season's wOBA better than actual wOBA does, so a big gap is mostly noise and likely to close.

In 2026, Jonny DeLuca, Ceddanne Rafaela, Andrew Vaughn, Nolan Arenado and Ty France beat their swings by 40 to 50 points. Salvador Perez, J.T. Realmuto, Corey Seager, Patrick Bailey and José Ramírez fell 30 to 35 points short. Use the filters to look at other seasons, and click any hitter to see their trend at the bottom of the page.

A gap that also showed up in a hitter's other seasons may be partly real skill, most often sprint speed, which neither expectation sees, or a slow catcher's lack of it. The table's prior gap column shows this. The best 2027 bounce-back bets are Ramírez, Guerrero, Witt and Grisham, whose gaps are new and whose swings are intact. Perez, Seager and Nootbaar have trailed their swings two or three years running, so expect only a partial rebound. The biggest regression risks are Vaughn, France and DeLuca, while part of the gap for fast runners like Simpson, Rafaela and Crow-Armstrong should stick.

Method, data and limits

  • Data comes from the Baseball Savant custom leaderboard: regular seasons 2024 to 2026, hitters with 150+ PA, 1,210 player-seasons, pulled November 21, 2026.
  • The aging analysis uses 603 consecutive-season pairs, weighted by the smaller of the two PA totals. "Expected" next-season xwOBA comes from a weighted regression on age and prior xwOBA.
  • The swing model is a ridge regression of wOBA on 13 swing, contact and discipline stats. Each season is predicted by a model trained only on the other two, so no hitter is graded against their own data.
  • Three seasons is a short window. Results past age 33 rest on only 34 pairs, and hitters who collapse drop out of the sample, which flatters the older groups.
  • Sprint speed and park effects are in neither expectation, which is why fast runners and some hitters in extreme parks show repeat gaps.
